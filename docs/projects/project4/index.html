Introduction

At the end of the year, which MSBA class are you most glad you took — and which could you have skipped? That is essentially the question behind this analysis.

MaxDiff (Best-Worst Scaling) is a survey method for measuring preference strength. Rather than asking students to rate each class on a 1–10 scale — a notoriously unreliable exercise, since people calibrate scales differently — MaxDiff shows each respondent a small set of items at a time and asks them to pick the most preferred and least preferred. Humans are much better at making trade-off comparisons than at assigning consistent numeric ratings. By repeating this across many screens, we can recover a stable preference ordering even with simple forced-choice questions.

In this survey, 85 MSBA students evaluated 10 core MSBA classes across 15 tasks each, with 4 classes shown per task. The goal is to estimate each class’s relative utility using three methods of increasing sophistication:

  1. Counts analysis — a simple score based on how often each class was picked best vs. worst
  2. MNL via Maximum Likelihood — a multinomial logit model fit by MLE, giving utility coefficients with standard errors
  3. MNL via Bayesian MCMC — the same model fit by Metropolis-Hastings sampling, giving full posterior distributions and credible intervals

All three methods should agree on the broad ranking — Marketing Analytics at the top, Analytics Consulting at the bottom — but they differ in how they quantify preference strength and communicate uncertainty.

Counts Analysis

The simplest way to summarize MaxDiff data is the counts score: for each class, compute the fraction of times it was chosen as best when shown, subtract the fraction of times it was chosen as worst, and you get a score ranging from −1 (always worst) to +1 (always best).

\[ \text{score}_j \;=\; \frac{\#\,\text{best}_j}{\#\,\text{shown}_j} \;-\; \frac{\#\,\text{worst}_j}{\#\,\text{shown}_j} \]

This is fast, transparent, and requires no model assumptions. Its main limitation is that it treats every “shown but not picked” event identically — there is no explicit model of the choice process. The MNL model in the next section addresses this.

From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

Before fitting a formal model, it helps to understand exactly how MaxDiff tasks map onto MNL choice observations.

In a standard discrete-choice experiment, a respondent sees a set of alternatives and picks one. The Multinomial Logit (MNL) model assigns each alternative \(j\) a utility \(\beta_j\), and the probability that alternative \(b\) is chosen from set \(\mathcal{S}\) is the softmax:

\[ P(\text{best} = b \mid \mathcal{S}) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \exp(\beta_j)} \]

MaxDiff generates two MNL observations per task:

  1. Best pick — a standard softmax over all 4 shown items, explained above.
  2. Worst pick — from the remaining 3 items (after removing the best), but with flipped utilities. Picking the worst item is the same as picking the “most disliked” item, so we flip the sign of every utility:

\[ P(\text{worst} = w \mid \mathcal{S} \setminus \{b\}) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_w)}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S} \setminus \{b\}} \exp(-\beta_j)} \]

Identifiability. The softmax is invariant to adding a constant to every \(\beta\) — shifting all utilities by \(c\) cancels out in the numerator and denominator. Only differences between utilities are identified. We fix this by setting \(\beta_1 = 0\) (MGTA 403 as the reference) and estimating \(\beta_2, \ldots, \beta_{10}\). All utility estimates should be interpreted relative to MGTA 403.

MNL via Maximum Likelihood

Log-Likelihood

Summing over all \(N \cdot T = 680\) tasks, the joint log-likelihood is:

\[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{\text{tasks}} \left[ \underbrace{\beta_{j^*_b} - \log \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \exp(\beta_j)}_{\text{best-pick contribution}} \;\underbrace{- \beta_{j^*_w} - \log \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S} \setminus \{j^*_b\}} \exp(-\beta_j)}_{\text{worst-pick contribution}} \right] \]

where \(j^*_b\) is the item chosen best and \(j^*_w\) is the item chosen worst. We implement the log-sum-exp trick for numerical stability: instead of computing \(\log \sum \exp(\beta_j)\), we subtract the maximum first.

Code 
def mnl_loglik(beta_9):
    """
    Log-likelihood for MaxDiff MNL.
    beta_9 : length-9 vector for items 2–10 (item 1 fixed at 0).
    """
    beta = np.concatenate([[0.0], beta_9])   # pin beta_1 = 0
    ll   = 0.0

    for task in tasks_data:
        items = task["items"]
        b     = task["best"]
        w     = task["worst"]

        # --- best pick: softmax over all 4 items ---
        u   = np.array([beta[i - 1] for i in items])
        m   = u.max()                                  # log-sum-exp trick
        ll += beta[b - 1] - (m + np.log(np.sum(np.exp(u - m))))

        # --- worst pick: softmax (flipped) over remaining 3 items ---
        rem  = [i for i in items if i != b]
        u_w  = np.array([-beta[i - 1] for i in rem])
        m_w  = u_w.max()
        ll  += -beta[w - 1] - (m_w + np.log(np.sum(np.exp(u_w - m_w))))

    return ll

The key conceptual difference: the counts score treats every “not-picked” event as equally uninformative. But an item that appears alongside three highly preferred items and still isn’t picked worst conveys something — namely, it’s not that bad. The MNL captures this context sensitivity by explicitly modeling the utility of every item in the consideration set, not just the chosen ones. This is why the MNL reranks SQL (up from #7 to #6) and Capstone (up from #6 to #7) relative to the counts method: both classes appear frequently alongside strong competitors, which makes their “not picked” events more informative than the raw counts suggest.

MNL via Bayesian Estimation

Maximum likelihood gives us point estimates and asymptotic standard errors. Bayesian estimation goes further: it produces a full posterior distribution over \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), which makes it easy to compute credible intervals for any derived quantity (like shares of preference) without needing the delta method.

The Posterior

By Bayes’ theorem:

\[ \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \text{data}) \;\propto\; \underbrace{L(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{likelihood}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{prior}} \]

We use a weakly informative prior: \(\boldsymbol{\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, 10 \cdot I)\), which places a standard deviation of \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2\) on each coefficient. This is intentionally broad — much wider than we expect the true values to be — so the prior has minimal influence and the data drive the posterior.

Metropolis-Hastings Sampler

We use a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. At each iteration, we propose a small random step in parameter space, compute the ratio of posterior densities (on the log scale, this is a difference), and accept the proposal with probability \(\min(1, e^{\Delta \log p})\).

Code 
def log_prior(beta_9):
    """Multivariate normal prior: N(0, 10·I)."""
    return -0.5 * np.sum(beta_9**2 / 10)

def log_posterior(beta_9):
    return mnl_loglik(beta_9) + log_prior(beta_9)

def metropolis_hastings(beta0, n_iter, step_sd):
    """
    Random-walk Metropolis sampler.
    Returns: (draws array [n_iter x K], acceptance rate)
    """
    K       = len(beta0)
    draws   = np.zeros((n_iter, K))
    beta    = beta0.copy()
    lp      = log_posterior(beta)
    n_accept = 0

    for t in range(n_iter):
        # Propose a random walk step
        beta_star = beta + np.random.randn(K) * step_sd
        lp_star   = log_posterior(beta_star)

        # Accept/reject (log scale for numerical stability)
        if np.log(np.random.rand()) < lp_star - lp:
            beta     = beta_star
            lp       = lp_star
            n_accept += 1

        draws[t] = beta

    return draws, n_accept / n_iter

The acceptance rate should land near 25–40%. Too low and the chain barely moves; too high and it takes tiny, highly correlated steps.

Where the methods agree. All three methods agree that Marketing Analytics (MGTA 495) is the clear #1 — it tops every ranking by a substantial margin. Where they disagree. The middle tier (ranks 3–8) shows some rank shuffling. For example, Capstone Project and SQL swap positions between the counts and MNL rankings. This is expected: the counts score treats every “not picked” event as neutral, while the MNL explicitly models the probability of each non-choice. In a task where Capstone appears alongside three highly preferred items, being “not picked” provides more evidence of low utility than if it appeared alongside three disliked items. The MNL captures this context sensitivity; the counts score does not.

What MNL adds over counts. The MNL models the complete choice process — the utility of every item in the consideration set informs the estimate for every other item. It also produces a clean probabilistic output (shares of preference) with proper standard errors. Counts are fast and intuitive but throw away information about the composition of each task’s choice set.

What Bayes adds over MLE. The posterior mean and MLE point estimates are nearly identical (as expected in large samples — the prior is diffuse and the likelihood dominates). The key advantage of Bayes is uncertainty on derived quantities. Posterior shares of preference — computed by pushing every MCMC draw through the softmax and averaging — come with credible intervals for free, no delta method required. This matters most for market-simulation exercises like “if we removed one class, how would demand redistribute?” — the full posterior lets us propagate uncertainty through any transformation.

For rigorous point estimates with classical inference, MLE is the right tool. What I’d explore next. The most natural extension would be a hierarchical (mixed) MNL that estimates individual-level utility coefficients, rather than treating all 85 students as identical. Even with this small sample, a hierarchical model would reveal whether the class rankings are driven by consensus preferences or by a few highly opinionated students. ## Introduction

At the end of the year, which MSBA class are you most glad you took — and which could you have skipped? That is essentially the question behind this analysis.

**MaxDiff** (Best-Worst Scaling) is a survey method for measuring preference strength. Rather than asking students to rate each class on a 1–10 scale — a notoriously unreliable exercise, since people calibrate scales differently — MaxDiff shows each respondent a small set of items at a time and asks them to pick the *most preferred* and *least preferred*. Humans are much better at making trade-off comparisons than at assigning consistent numeric ratings. By repeating this across many screens, we can recover a stable preference ordering even with simple forced-choice questions.

In this survey, **85 MSBA students** evaluated **10 core MSBA classes** across **15 tasks each**, with 4 classes shown per task. The goal is to estimate each class's relative utility using three methods of increasing sophistication:

1. **Counts analysis** — a simple score based on how often each class was picked best vs. worst
2. **MNL via Maximum Likelihood** — a multinomial logit model fit by MLE, giving utility coefficients with standard errors
3. **MNL via Bayesian MCMC** — the same model fit by Metropolis-Hastings sampling, giving full posterior distributions and credible intervals

All three methods should agree on the broad ranking — Marketing Analytics at the top, Analytics Consulting at the bottom — but they differ in how they quantify preference strength and communicate uncertainty.

## Counts Analysis

The simplest way to summarize MaxDiff data is the **counts score**: for each class, compute the fraction of times it was chosen as *best* when shown, subtract the fraction of times it was chosen as *worst*, and you get a score ranging from −1 (always worst) to +1 (always best).

$$
\text{score}_j \;=\; \frac{\#\,\text{best}_j}{\#\,\text{shown}_j} \;-\; \frac{\#\,\text{worst}_j}{\#\,\text{shown}_j}
$$

This is fast, transparent, and requires no model assumptions. Its main limitation is that it treats every "shown but not picked" event identically — there is no explicit model of the choice process. The MNL model in the next section addresses this.

## From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

Before fitting a formal model, it helps to understand exactly how MaxDiff tasks map onto MNL choice observations.

In a standard discrete-choice experiment, a respondent sees a set of alternatives and picks one. The **Multinomial Logit (MNL)** model assigns each alternative $j$ a utility $\beta_j$, and the probability that alternative $b$ is chosen from set $\mathcal{S}$ is the **softmax**:

$$
P(\text{best} = b \mid \mathcal{S}) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \exp(\beta_j)}
$$

MaxDiff generates *two* MNL observations per task:

1. **Best pick** — a standard softmax over all 4 shown items, explained above.
2. **Worst pick** — from the remaining 3 items (after removing the best), but with *flipped* utilities. Picking the worst item is the same as picking the "most disliked" item, so we flip the sign of every utility:

$$
P(\text{worst} = w \mid \mathcal{S} \setminus \{b\}) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_w)}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S} \setminus \{b\}} \exp(-\beta_j)}
$$

**Identifiability.** The softmax is invariant to adding a constant to every $\beta$ — shifting all utilities by $c$ cancels out in the numerator and denominator. Only *differences* between utilities are identified. We fix this by setting $\beta_1 = 0$ (MGTA 403 as the reference) and estimating $\beta_2, \ldots, \beta_{10}$. All utility estimates should be interpreted relative to MGTA 403.

## MNL via Maximum Likelihood

### Log-Likelihood

Summing over all $N \cdot T = 680$ tasks, the joint log-likelihood is:

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{\text{tasks}} \left[
  \underbrace{\beta_{j^*_b} - \log \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \exp(\beta_j)}_{\text{best-pick contribution}}
  \;\underbrace{- \beta_{j^*_w} - \log \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S} \setminus \{j^*_b\}} \exp(-\beta_j)}_{\text{worst-pick contribution}}
\right]
$$

where $j^*_b$ is the item chosen best and $j^*_w$ is the item chosen worst. We implement the **log-sum-exp trick** for numerical stability: instead of computing $\log \sum \exp(\beta_j)$, we subtract the maximum first.

```{python}
#| echo: true

def mnl_loglik(beta_9):
    """
    Log-likelihood for MaxDiff MNL.
    beta_9 : length-9 vector for items 2–10 (item 1 fixed at 0).
    """
    beta = np.concatenate([[0.0], beta_9])   # pin beta_1 = 0
    ll   = 0.0

    for task in tasks_data:
        items = task["items"]
        b     = task["best"]
        w     = task["worst"]

        # --- best pick: softmax over all 4 items ---
        u   = np.array([beta[i - 1] for i in items])
        m   = u.max()                                  # log-sum-exp trick
        ll += beta[b - 1] - (m + np.log(np.sum(np.exp(u - m))))

        # --- worst pick: softmax (flipped) over remaining 3 items ---
        rem  = [i for i in items if i != b]
        u_w  = np.array([-beta[i - 1] for i in rem])
        m_w  = u_w.max()
        ll  += -beta[w - 1] - (m_w + np.log(np.sum(np.exp(u_w - m_w))))

    return ll

print(f"Log-likelihood at all-zero beta : {mnl_loglik(np.zeros(9)):.2f}")
```

## MNL via Bayesian Estimation

Maximum likelihood gives us point estimates and asymptotic standard errors. **Bayesian estimation** goes further: it produces a full **posterior distribution** over $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, which makes it easy to compute credible intervals for any derived quantity (like shares of preference) without needing the delta method.

### The Posterior

By Bayes' theorem:

$$
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \text{data}) \;\propto\; \underbrace{L(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{likelihood}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{prior}}
$$

We use a **weakly informative prior**: $\boldsymbol{\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, 10 \cdot I)$, which places a standard deviation of $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$ on each coefficient. This is intentionally broad — much wider than we expect the true values to be — so the prior has minimal influence and the data drive the posterior.

### Metropolis-Hastings Sampler

We use a **random-walk Metropolis-Hastings** algorithm. At each iteration, we propose a small random step in parameter space, compute the ratio of posterior densities (on the log scale, this is a difference), and accept the proposal with probability $\min(1, e^{\Delta \log p})$.

```{python}
#| echo: true

def log_prior(beta_9):
    """Multivariate normal prior: N(0, 10·I)."""
    return -0.5 * np.sum(beta_9**2 / 10)

def log_posterior(beta_9):
    return mnl_loglik(beta_9) + log_prior(beta_9)

def metropolis_hastings(beta0, n_iter, step_sd):
    """
    Random-walk Metropolis sampler.
    Returns: (draws array [n_iter x K], acceptance rate)
    """
    K       = len(beta0)
    draws   = np.zeros((n_iter, K))
    beta    = beta0.copy()
    lp      = log_posterior(beta)
    n_accept = 0

    for t in range(n_iter):
        # Propose a random walk step
        beta_star = beta + np.random.randn(K) * step_sd
        lp_star   = log_posterior(beta_star)

        # Accept/reject (log scale for numerical stability)
        if np.log(np.random.rand()) < lp_star - lp:
            beta     = beta_star
            lp       = lp_star
            n_accept += 1

        draws[t] = beta

    return draws, n_accept / n_iter
```

### Discussion

**Where the methods agree.** All three methods agree that **Marketing Analytics (MGTA 495) is the clear #1** — it tops every ranking by a substantial margin. At the other end, Analytics Consulting (MGTA 444) and AI Math & Analytics (MGTA 403) consistently occupy the bottom two positions. When a preference signal is strong, even the simplest counts analysis captures it correctly.

**Where they disagree.** The middle tier (ranks 3–8) shows some rank shuffling. For example, Capstone Project and SQL swap positions between the counts and MNL rankings. This is expected: the counts score treats every "not picked" event as neutral, while the MNL explicitly models the probability of each non-choice. In a task where Capstone appears alongside three highly preferred items, being "not picked" provides more evidence of low utility than if it appeared alongside three disliked items. The MNL captures this **context sensitivity**; the counts score does not.

**What MNL adds over counts.** The MNL models the complete choice process — the utility of every item in the consideration set informs the estimate for every other item. It also produces a clean probabilistic output (shares of preference) with proper standard errors. Counts are fast and intuitive but throw away information about the composition of each task's choice set.

**What Bayes adds over MLE.** The posterior mean and MLE point estimates are nearly identical (as expected in large samples — the prior is diffuse and the likelihood dominates). The key advantage of Bayes is **uncertainty on derived quantities**. Posterior shares of preference — computed by pushing every MCMC draw through the softmax and averaging — come with credible intervals for free, no delta method required. This matters most for market-simulation exercises like "if we removed one class, how would demand redistribute?" — the full posterior lets us propagate uncertainty through any transformation.

## Discussion

**For a student or academic advisor.** The MSBA cohort has a clear favorite: **Marketing Analytics** (MGTA 495). Whether due to its content, instructor, or career relevance, students rank it far above every other class — its share of preference is nearly double that of the second-place Business Analytics (MGTA 453). Three classes form a competitive middle tier: Business Analytics, Customer Analytics, and Large Data, all with similar and positive utilities. At the bottom, Analytics Consulting and AI Math & Analytics are the least preferred by the cohort, though it's worth noting that "least preferred" in an MNL sense means students are less likely to choose them over alternatives — not that they find them unhelpful.

**The methodological scorecard.** For a quick executive summary — "which three classes do students like most?" — the counts analysis is perfectly adequate. For rigorous point estimates with classical inference, MLE is the right tool. For any exercise that requires uncertainty on a *function* of the parameters (shares, predicted market shares, "what if" simulations), or if you plan to extend the model hierarchically to individual-level preferences, Bayesian MCMC is the clear choice.

**Caveats.** This survey was completed by a self-selected sample of current MSBA students — 85 respondents, all of whom have some experience with these classes. Preferences may differ by cohort year, prior background, or career goals. Students who haven't taken a class yet may respond based on reputation rather than experience, which could distort the rankings. Future work could collect pre- and post-course ratings, or segment respondents by cohort/concentration, to understand *which* students prefer *which* classes and why.

**What I'd explore next.** The most natural extension would be a **hierarchical (mixed) MNL** that estimates individual-level utility coefficients, rather than treating all 85 students as identical. Even with this small sample, a hierarchical model would reveal whether the class rankings are driven by consensus preferences or by a few highly opinionated students. A second avenue would be to re-run this analysis after the cohort completes all 10 classes, to see whether post-experience preferences align with pre-experience expectations — or whether students systematically misjudge which classes they'll find most valuable.
